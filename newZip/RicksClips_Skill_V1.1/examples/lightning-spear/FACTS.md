## 2. The principle as spoken (rows 16-17)

Spoken: "lightning always takes the easiest road to the ground, and his iron wire was a road."

Verdict: PARTLY. Honest for children in spirit: a rod and its wire give lightning a low-impedance route to the ground (Wikipedia Lightning rod, opened). It does not make a rod draw extra lightning from afar. What a pedant would say: lightning does not literally always take the single least-resistance path (it branches, it can side-flash, it is steered by the leader that reaches down from the cloud), and "always" is an absolute. "Easiest road" and "road" are fine as a child's word.

Safe wording at the same length (9 words for 9 words): "lightning looks for the easiest road to the ground,". The picture of the bolt hesitating at a crossroads (row 16) matches this wording better than "always takes". The second half "and his iron wire was a road" stays.

Nothing in the spoken text says a rod attracts more lightning, so nothing false is said about attraction.

## 3. Period truth, northern Italy about 1765

- Rods known in Italy before 1765: the idea was known (Wikipedia Toaldo: Toaldo adopted Franklin's views against the French school of Nollet; his 1772 treatise and 1774 pamphlet "contributed largely to remove the popular prejudices of the time"). SNIPPET (Popular Science Monthly 31): Beccaria introduced Franklin's invention to Italy, "but St. Mark's in Venice went unprotected until 1766". That 1766 conflicts with 1776 on St Mark's own pages (section 5). I could not open an Italian first-rod source, so "first rod in Italy: when, where" is NOT FOUND. Installations found: Siena Cathedral (no date), St Mark's tower Venice (1776), Padua public observatory conductor (described in Toaldo's 1774 book, SNIPPET). A lone mason with a rod about 1765 is therefore early, not provably impossible. Fine for a fable because no year is spoken or shown. Safer story-clock note for the lead: say "about 1770" in the notes, never on screen.
- Church and rods: Toaldo's rods stood on Siena Cathedral and St Mark's (Wikipedia Toaldo, opened), so the Church did accept rods, which fits the Priest's turn at the end. The earlier checker's remark that the Church first objected then relented was NOT confirmed in this pass.
- Bells rung against storms: CONFIRMED as a European belief (Wikipedia Bell-ringer, opened), NOT FOUND as an Italian page. Fine.
- Counting seconds: see item 5 above. PARTLY.
- Thumb-thick iron: PARTLY plausible, item 6 above.
- "Spear" for a pointed iron rod on a tower: a pointed rod above the roof was exactly Franklin's design (the upper end "sharpened to a fine Point", pointed vs ball-ended rods were argued over in the 1770s; Wikipedia Lightning rod, opened). Looks like a spear to a child; it is a child's word, fine. The "bracket" that holds it is a story detail.
- A gold angel on a tower top is period-true: St Mark's Campanile got a gilded-copper rotating angel in 1513 (basilicasanmarco.it, opened).

## 4. Picture facts: what a strike does to an unprotected tower

- 11 August 1489, St Mark's Campanile, Venice: "lightning again struck the tower during a violent storm, setting ablaze the spire." "The bells fell to the floor of the belfry and the masonry of the tower itself cracked." (imagesofvenice.com, opened.) The same page: damage over the 14th to 15th centuries was "mostly due to lightning strikes on the top wooden structures".
- Official basilica site (opened): after the copper-covered wooden top was built the tower became "an irresistible attraction for lightning"; in 1489 "it was torn off by a bolt of lightning, severely shaking the tower"; in 1745 a strike "tore away the corner facing the Clock Tower".
- So the real pattern is: stone cracks or loses a corner, the wooden spire / roof and bell frame burn, bells fall. A marble shaft itself does not burn. Your row 21 picture (the wooden roof burns with one tall flame on a white shaft, a black crack down the marble) is correct. The crack in row 20 is documented (1489). Bells falling is a possible extra beat.

## 5. Optional Venice line

Line: "In Venice, lightning hit one bell tower for almost four hundred years, until it got a spear too."

- Rod installed 1776, the first in Venice by Giuseppe Toaldo: CONFIRMED by three sources. Wikipedia (earlier checker): 18 March 1776. imagesofvenice.com (opened): "18 March 1776 ... the physicist Giuseppe Toaldo, Professor of Astronomy at the University of Padua, installed a lightning rod; the first in Venice." Official basilicasanmarco.it (opened): "on 18 May 1776, Abbot Giuseppe Toaldo" placed a rod on the cusp, "to the great amazement of the entire city"; after this "lightning strikes no longer caused any damage". The sources disagree on month (March or May), so say no month.
- Strikes from 1388: Wikipedia list (earlier checker) starts 7 June 1388; Campaners page (SNIPPET) gives 1388, 1417, 1489 and others. 1388 to 1776 = 388 years: "almost four hundred" is fair.
- "until it got a spear too": the official site says damage stopped, not that strikes stopped. The line claims no more, so it is safe. Better: "lightning hit one bell tower again and again for almost four hundred years" (+3 words) because the list is about a dozen strikes, not a continuous hit.
- Not needed, one conflict to know about: SNIPPET sources (Popular Science Monthly, a Trento PDF) give 1766 for a rod; the two St Mark's pages give 1776. Do not say any year.
- Length: the line adds 18 words, so the film becomes 250 words, over the 200-235 band and about 64 s of speech. It is optional; the fable works without it.

## SAFE AS FABLE

- Unnamed mason, invented priest, rival, bell ringer, town; "hill town in northern Italy" with no year spoken.
- "Iron wire, thick as a thumb" (plausible between Franklin's "small iron rod" and the 1769 St Paul's iron bar; staples and a foot "in the moist Ground" match Franklin 1753). The word "wire" for a thumb-thick conductor is a child's word.
- "Spear" for the pointed rod; the pointed tip is Franklin's design.
- Bells rung against storms and the Priest's belief "the bell scares it off" (a documented European belief, with deaths of ringers in France; the film shows no deaths).
- The town counting ten, five, one (method existed since the 1600s; the ordinary-people counting is the fable).
- The bell ringer lifted off the floor, the toothpick gag, the angry hen, the steam puff, the bent trumpet, the hat sliding off.
- A gold angel on a tall tower (St Mark's had a gilded angel from 1513); a strike that cracks the stone and burns the wooden top (1489).
- The Priest ordering spears for every roof (Siena Cathedral and St Mark's did get rods; the Church accepted them).
- Rod and wire shown as a safe road for lightning with the tower untouched.
- Nobody dies or is hurt; no Franklin name, Brescia, gunpowder.
